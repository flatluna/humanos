> **Humans First. AI Agents Second.**
>
> Human OS uses artificial intelligence to help people become more capable, not more dependent. The purpose is not simply to give an answer. The purpose is to help each person understand, remember, practice, judge, and act with greater confidence.

## What is Human OS?

Human OS is a learning and professional-growth environment for students, employees, professors, managers, and organizations.

It brings together several activities that are often separated:

- learning new knowledge;
- practicing important conversations and decisions;
- remembering what has been learned;
- understanding personal strengths and areas for growth;
- connecting learning with a real role, project, or goal; and
- collecting evidence of progress over time.

The experience is personal. Human OS can adapt to a person's interests, responsibilities, experience, learning needs, professional goals, and organizational context.

The central question is not only **"What answer can the system give me?"** It is also:

> **"What can I understand, remember, explain, and do for myself?"**

## Why Human OS is different

Many digital learning experiences encourage people to read, watch, click, and move on. Human OS places more emphasis on active participation.

A learner is invited to:

1. think about a question before seeing an explanation;
2. learn from a clear and relevant explanation;
3. recall the important idea without looking at the answer;
4. use the idea in a realistic situation; and
5. receive feedback about what is strong and what should improve.

This approach helps knowledge become usable capability. The goal is not to finish more pages. The goal is to become more prepared and more independent.

## The main parts of Human OS

## 1. Personal profile

The personal profile gives Human OS the context needed to make learning relevant.

Depending on the person's situation, it may include:

- their name and background;
- their academic or professional experience;
- their current role or area of responsibility;
- their interests and objectives;
- their résumé or professional history;
- the skills needed in their role; and
- projects, work processes, or subjects they want to develop.

This context helps the experience feel like a conversation with someone who understands the learner's situation, rather than a generic course for an anonymous audience.

## 2. CQI Workspace and HermanScience report

The CQI Workspace helps a person understand their own way of approaching decisions, work, learning, and relationships.

A person can upload their HermanScience or CQI report and review a clear, organized profile. The workspace presents the report in a practical way, including:

- the person's main pattern or profile;
- important strengths and natural tendencies;
- possible challenges or blind spots;
- situations in which the pattern may be especially useful; and
- an anchor foundation for turning self-knowledge into action.

The report is not a label or a limit. It is a starting point for reflection. It can help a learner ask better questions, choose useful development goals, and understand why certain situations feel natural or difficult.

The most valuable use of the CQI Workspace is to connect personal insight with practice. For example, a person may notice that they are strong at analysis but want to become more comfortable speaking with customers. They can then use Human OS to practice that situation and observe their progress.

## 3. Engram Academy and learning journeys

Engram Academy is the learning experience for building knowledge and lasting understanding.

An **Engram** is a meaningful unit of knowledge or capability that a person can understand, recall, and use. It is more than a video, a page, or a completed activity.

Learning journeys may include:

- professional subjects;
- academic subjects;
- company knowledge;
- policies and procedures;
- communication skills;
- leadership and management;
- technical or specialized knowledge; and
- preparation for a specific role or responsibility.

Each subject is organized into manageable ideas so that the learner can build understanding step by step. Related ideas are connected, helping the learner see how one concept supports another.

## The six steps of a learning experience

Human OS guides the learner through six connected stages:

## 1. Before You Begin

Before starting, the tutor considers the learner's profile, role, experience, and objective. The learner is invited to think about what they already know and what they expect to learn.

This gives the lesson a meaningful starting point instead of treating every learner as if they were beginning from the same place.

## 2. Teaching

The learner receives a clear explanation of the subject. The tutor introduces the important ideas, examples, language, and relationships needed to understand the topic.

The explanation is adapted to the learner's level and purpose.

## 3. Going Deeper

The learner goes beyond the first explanation. They explore examples, ask questions, compare ideas, and examine how the subject works in more detail.

This step helps transform a first impression into a stronger and more complete understanding.

## 4. In Your Role

The learner applies the subject to their own role, studies, project, or professional situation.

For an employee, this may mean connecting an idea to a real work process. For a student, it may mean applying it to a thesis, assignment, experiment, or future career. The goal is to make the learning useful outside the lesson.

## 5. Recall

The tutor asks the learner to bring the important ideas back from memory without immediately showing the explanation again.

The learner may explain the concept, answer a question, describe a process, or give an example. Evocation helps reveal what the learner truly understands and strengthens long-term memory.

## 6. Evaluation

The learner demonstrates what they can do. The tutor reviews the response and gives feedback about strengths, missing pieces, and what to practice next.

Evaluation is not only a final score. It is a useful conversation about progress and the next step toward greater independence.

Together, these stages move the learner from preparation to understanding, from understanding to application, and from application to lasting capability.

## 4. Courses and learning content

Courses can begin with many kinds of source material, such as a book, a professor's notes, a company manual, a policy, a process description, a résumé, or a professional guide.

Human OS turns that material into a guided learning experience. The learner is not expected to absorb everything at once. Instead, the experience helps them move from:

- initial curiosity;
- to understanding;
- to memory;
- to practical use; and
- to demonstrated ability.

The material remains connected to its original subject, so the learner can trust that the explanations and activities are related to what they are expected to learn.

## 5. The learning conversation

A learner can interact with Human OS through reading, writing, or natural conversation. The experience may ask questions, invite an explanation, present a situation, or request a decision.

The purpose of these conversations is to make the learner think. Human OS can offer guidance and feedback, but the learner remains the person who must explain, decide, practice, and produce the work.

This makes the experience useful for people who learn by asking questions, speaking ideas aloud, testing their understanding, and receiving immediate feedback.

## 6. Studio Labs

Studio Labs are practice spaces for situations that matter in real life.

A Lab presents a realistic situation and another person to speak with. The other person may be an interviewer, customer, manager, colleague, student, committee member, or business leader. The learner must respond as they would in the real situation.

The purpose is not to memorize a script. The purpose is to practice judgment, communication, listening, preparation, and response.

A person can repeat a Lab, try a different approach, and learn from each attempt. This creates a private space to prepare before an important real conversation.

## Examples of Studio Labs

## Employer interview practice

A learner can practice an interview for a particular kind of role.

The interviewer can ask about:

- previous experience;
- accomplishments and responsibilities;
- technical knowledge;
- teamwork and leadership;
- difficult situations;
- decisions the learner has made; and
- how the learner would respond to a situation in the new role.

The practice can be connected to the learner's actual background and professional goals. This makes the conversation more useful than rehearsing general interview questions.

## Thesis and academic defense practice

A student can practice defending a thesis, research project, report, or important academic work before an examination or presentation.

The academic committee may ask about:

- the central problem;
- the purpose of the work;
- the main contribution;
- the questions being studied;
- the approach and methods used;
- the evidence and results;
- limitations;
- ethical considerations; and
- possible future work.

The student must explain and defend their own work. The Lab does not replace the student's preparation. It gives the student a demanding and realistic opportunity to organize ideas, answer questions, and gain confidence.

## Customer conversation practice

A learner can practice speaking with a customer or prospective customer.

The customer may have a need, concern, objection, or problem that is not immediately obvious. The learner must ask good questions, listen carefully, understand the situation, and respond appropriately.

This can be useful for practicing:

- discovering customer needs;
- presenting a solution;
- handling an upset customer;
- providing customer support;
- negotiating price, timing, or scope;
- explaining a report or recommendation; and
- building trust.

The customer does not simply provide the correct answer. The learner must discover what matters through the conversation.

## Other professional practice situations

Studio Labs can also support practice for:

- performance reviews;
- difficult feedback;
- conflict resolution;
- onboarding and training;
- executive presentations;
- sales discovery;
- requirements conversations;
- career mentoring;
- teaching a process or subject;
- business updates to a manager; and
- workplace customer support.

The situation can be adapted to the learner's role, industry, experience, and development goals.

## What happens after a practice session?

After a Lab, the learner can review the conversation and receive useful feedback.

The review may include:

- an overall result;
- strengths demonstrated during the conversation;
- areas that need improvement;
- specific moments from the conversation;
- the quality of individual answers;
- suggestions for a stronger response; and
- skills that would be useful to practice next.

This turns practice into a learning cycle:

> **Prepare → Practice → Review → Improve → Practice again**

The learner can also look back at previous attempts and see how their performance changes over time.

## Skills and capabilities

Human OS distinguishes between knowing something and being able to use it.

A capability may include several kinds of growth:

- understanding an idea;
- remembering it without help;
- explaining it clearly;
- applying it to a new situation;
- making a sound decision;
- communicating with another person;
- performing a professional process; and
- working independently.

Skills can be connected to a person's role, studies, projects, or goals. This helps learners see why a subject matters and how it connects to their future.

For example, a person may not only study negotiation as an abstract subject. They may practice negotiating with a customer, receive feedback, identify a weakness in their preparation, and return to a related learning activity.

## Goals, projects, and growth plans

Human OS helps connect learning with direction.

A learner may define goals such as:

- preparing for a new role;
- improving a professional skill;
- completing a thesis;
- becoming more confident with customers;
- leading a team more effectively;
- learning a new subject; or
- preparing for a career transition.

Projects give those goals a practical setting. A project can provide the evidence that a capability is being used in the real world.

A growth plan brings together the person's goals, learning activities, practice sessions, and next steps. It helps answer:

- What am I trying to improve?
- Why does it matter?
- What should I learn?
- What should I practice?
- What evidence will show progress?
- What should I do next?

## Evidence and progress

Progress in Human OS is not based only on finishing activities. It can also be demonstrated through evidence.

Evidence may include:

- an answer to a learning question;
- a completed practice conversation;
- a presentation or explanation;
- a project result;
- feedback from a professor, manager, or colleague;
- a successful application of a process; or
- an improvement between two attempts.

This creates a richer picture of development. A learner can see not only what they have studied, but also what they can now explain and do.

## Recall and long-term memory

Human OS gives special importance to recall: bringing an idea back from memory without immediately looking at the explanation.

Recall helps a learner discover what they truly understand and what still needs attention. It also makes knowledge more available when the learner is under pressure in an exam, meeting, interview, or customer conversation.

A person may return to important ideas after time has passed. This gives them the opportunity to strengthen knowledge that is beginning to fade, rather than waiting until everything has been forgotten.

## My Brain

**My Brain** is a personal map of the knowledge the learner has already studied and mastered. Each bright neuron represents an Engram: a meaningful memory of an idea, skill, process, or subject.

The purpose of My Brain is to answer a practical question:

> **What do I know right now, how strong is that memory, and what should I review next?**

It is not a test that judges the learner as a person. It is a living picture of knowledge that changes as the learner studies, recalls, reviews, and applies ideas.

## What the symbols and colors mean

- **Each neuron or circle:** One Engram that the learner has learned and passed.
- **The colored ring:** How fresh the memory is at this moment.
- **Strong:** The idea is likely to be remembered easily.
- **Fading:** The idea is beginning to weaken and would benefit from a review.
- **Almost forgotten:** The idea needs attention soon so it can be recovered and strengthened.
- **The percentage:** The estimated likelihood that the learner could recall the idea if asked right now.
- **The Automatic badge:** The learner has passed five full reviews of the topic. The knowledge has become much more natural, similar to a skill that can be used without consciously rebuilding every step.

The colors and percentages are meant to guide attention. They do not define intelligence or ability. A fading memory is a normal part of learning, and reviewing it is how the learner makes it stronger again.

## The memory model

Human OS estimates memory strength using the natural forgetting pattern of human memory. The estimate considers:

- how long it has been since the last review;
- how many times the learner has reviewed the idea;
- whether the learner successfully recalled and applied it; and
- whether the idea has become stable through repeated successful reviews.

The estimate helps Human OS decide which knowledge is fresh and which knowledge deserves another opportunity to be recalled. It is a guide for learning, not a permanent judgment. A learner can improve the strength of an Engram by returning to it and successfully bringing it back from memory.

## The My Brain summary

At the top of the page, the learner can see a short summary of their knowledge:

- **Active Engrams:** The number of mastered memory units currently being followed.
- **Knowledge Areas:** The number of courses or subject areas represented in the brain map.
- **Due for Review:** The number of Engrams that need attention soon.
- **Global Memory Strength:** An overall estimate of how fresh the learner's mastered knowledge is right now.

For example, a learner may see 14 Active Engrams, 2 Knowledge Areas, 1 Due for Review, and 86% Global Memory Strength. This means the learner has 14 mastered ideas across two areas, one of them needs review, and the overall knowledge is currently estimated to be quite fresh.

## Active Learning Phase

During the first phase, **Learning**, the learner is building their personal brain map.

The best action in this phase is to continue with the available courses. Human OS will identify when an important idea needs a quick review. The learner does not need to study everything at once or memorize the entire map in one sitting.

The experience can begin with one selected neuron. The learner chooses an idea and discusses it with the Agent, explains what they remember, asks questions, and strengthens that particular memory.

## Grid and Graph views

The brain map can be understood in two complementary ways:

- **Grid view:** Shows Engrams as an organized list that is easy to scan, filter, and compare.
- **Graph view:** Shows how ideas connect within and across subject areas, helping the learner see the larger structure of what they know.

The learner can filter the map by course, memory status, or review date. For example, they can view all courses, focus on one course, show only mastered topics, or show only topics that need review.

## Understanding an Engram

When the learner selects an Engram, they can see:

- the course or knowledge area it belongs to;
- the title of the idea;
- the current recall percentage;
- when it was last reviewed;
- whether the memory is strong, fading, or almost forgotten; and
- whether it has reached Automatic status.

The learner can then talk with the Agent about that specific neuron. This makes review focused and personal. Instead of asking the learner to repeat an entire course, Human OS helps them work on the exact idea that needs attention.

## Why My Brain matters

My Brain changes learning from a list of completed lessons into an understanding of usable knowledge.

It helps the learner:

- see what has genuinely been mastered;
- notice knowledge that is beginning to fade;
- review before an important idea is lost;
- connect ideas from different courses;
- understand the difference between recent learning and stable memory; and
- build confidence based on what they can actually recall and use.

The goal is not to make every memory show 100% all the time. The goal is to maintain the knowledge that matters, recover ideas when they begin to fade, and gradually turn important learning into dependable capability.

**My Achievements** is the part of Human OS that helps learners see and celebrate their progress. It is not a competition with other people. It is a personal record of effort, understanding, consistency, and completed learning.

The page shows:

- how many achievements have been earned;
- which achievements are close to being unlocked;
- progress toward achievements that are still in progress;
- the date an achievement was earned; and
- the reason each achievement matters.

When the app shows a progress value such as **9 / 10**, it means the learner has completed 9 of the 10 actions required for that achievement. A percentage such as **90%** shows how close the learner is to unlocking it. A locked achievement is not a failure. It is a clear description of the next possible milestone.

## Effort Milestones

This category measures the total number of Engrams mastered across the learner's available courses.

- **First Neuron:** Mastered the first Engram. This marks the beginning of the learning journey.
- **Apprentice:** Mastered 5% of the available Engrams.
- **Practitioner:** Mastered 15% of the available Engrams.
- **Expert:** Mastered 30% of the available Engrams.
- **Master:** Mastered 50% of the available Engrams.
- **Scholar:** Mastered 75% of the available Engrams.
- **Legend:** Mastered 100% of the available Engrams.

These targets are personal. They are based on the learning content assigned to that learner, so the goal reflects their own learning journey rather than an identical target for everyone.

## Course Progress

This category measures progress within one individual course. A learner can earn these achievements more easily by continuing one course until the next milestone is reached.

- **Quarter Way:** Completed 25% of a course.
- **Halfway There:** Completed 50% of a course.
- **Home Stretch:** Completed 75% of a course.
- **Skill Mastered:** Completed 100% of a course.

These achievements recognize the stages of completing one course. Completing part of a course is valuable, but completing the full course shows that the learner stayed with the complete learning journey.

## Skills Completed

This category measures how many complete career or role-related courses the learner has finished.

- **First Skill:** Completed the first required career course.
- **Getting Started:** Completed 15% of the assigned career courses.
- **Building Momentum:** Completed 30% of the assigned career courses.
- **Halfway Expert:** Completed 50% of the assigned career courses.
- **Almost There:** Completed 75% of the assigned career courses.
- **Skill Master:** Completed 100% of the assigned career courses.

The difference between **Course Progress** and **Skills Completed** is simple: Course Progress follows progress inside one course, while Skills Completed follows the number of complete courses finished across the learner's wider development plan.

## Mastery and Quality

This category recognizes the quality and consistency of the learner's understanding, not only the amount of content completed.

- **Perfectionist:** Earned a perfect score of 100% in 10 assessment rounds.
- **One-Shot:** Mastered 10 Engrams on the first attempt without needing a retry.
- **Flawless:** Mastered 5 Engrams in a row on the first attempt with strong scores.

These achievements encourage careful understanding, preparation, and accurate application rather than rushing through activities.

## Role Challenges

- **Critical Guardian:** Mastered 10 critical-tier Engrams that are especially important for the learner's role.

This achievement helps draw attention to knowledge that has a high impact on the learner's responsibilities. Not every topic has the same importance for every person, so this category connects learning with the person's role.

## Habit and Consistency

This category recognizes the habit of returning to learning regularly and strengthening memory over time.

- **7-Day Streak:** Learned on 7 consecutive days.
- **30-Day Streak:** Learned on 30 consecutive days.
- **100-Day Streak:** Learned on 100 consecutive days.
- **Memory Rescuer:** Recovered 10 ideas that were beginning to fade by actively recalling them again.
- **Autopilot:** Coming soon. This will recognize 5 Engrams that have become strongly established in long-term memory.

A streak is not about studying for a certain number of hours. It recognizes returning to meaningful learning each day. The Memory Rescuer achievement shows that forgetting is not a failure: noticing that memory is fading and recovering it is part of learning well.

## Luck and Surprises

Human OS may occasionally provide a surprise reward while a learner is making progress.

- **Lucky:** Received one rare 3.0x Jackpot reward multiplier.
- **Triple Luck:** Received three Jackpot reward multipliers.

These rewards are unexpected bonuses. They do not replace learning or determine mastery. They simply add an element of celebration to the experience.

## Points, levels, and streaks

When a learner completes an Engram, Human OS may award points. Points contribute to a learner's level and can include a normal reward or an occasional surprise multiplier.

The learning profile can also show:

- the current level;
- total points;
- points needed for the next level;
- current and longest learning streaks;
- available streak protection; and
- total Engrams mastered.

The levels are a friendly way to represent continued growth. The important result is not the number itself. The important result is the knowledge, memory, confidence, and capability built along the way.

## How to read the Achievements page

The learner can use the page in four simple ways:

1. **Celebrate earned achievements.** Notice what has already been accomplished.
2. **Look at “Close to Unlocking.”** Choose a realistic next milestone.
3. **Open “What does this mean?”** Read why the achievement matters and what action completes it.
4. **Use the progress as guidance.** Return to learning, recall, or practice rather than treating the badge as the final goal.

Achievements are reminders of real learning behavior. They recognize starting, continuing, remembering, applying, completing, and returning to improve.

Students can use Human OS to:

- understand difficult subjects;
- prepare for exams and presentations;
- practice explaining their ideas;
- defend a thesis or research project;
- connect academic learning with professional situations;
- prepare for interviews and internships; and
- build confidence through repeated, private practice.

The student remains responsible for the quality and honesty of their work. Human OS is a tutor, practice partner, and source of feedback, not a substitute for study or academic judgment.

## Human OS for employees and business people

Employees and business professionals can use Human OS to:

- learn the knowledge required for their role;
- practice important workplace conversations;
- prepare for a new responsibility;
- improve customer and stakeholder relationships;
- strengthen leadership and communication;
- understand company processes;
- identify useful next skills; and
- connect professional growth with real business needs.

Organizations can use the same approach to support readiness, internal mobility, engagement, and retention. The emphasis is on building people who can perform well and think independently, not simply on distributing more information.

## Human OS for professors and educators

Professors can use Human OS as a companion to teaching.

It can help students prepare before class, practice explanations after class, revisit important concepts, and test their ability to apply knowledge. A professor can use realistic conversations and assessments to see whether students understand an idea deeply enough to use it.

Human OS does not remove the importance of the professor. It gives students more opportunities to practice between meetings and gives educators another way to encourage reflection, application, and evidence of learning.

## AI as a learning partner

Human OS uses AI to make learning and practice more personal and responsive.

The AI can act as:

- a tutor who asks questions;
- a conversation partner;
- an interviewer;
- a customer;
- a manager;
- an academic examiner;
- a coach who gives feedback; or
- a guide who helps the learner choose a next step.

The AI's role is to support human capability. It should encourage the learner to think, explain, decide, and act rather than doing all of the thinking for them.

Important decisions, academic judgments, and professional responsibilities remain with people.

## A practical Human OS journey

A learner might use Human OS in the following way:

1. **Understand yourself.** Review your personal profile and, when available, your CQI or HermanScience report.
2. **Choose a meaningful goal.** Decide what you want to learn, improve, or prepare for.
3. **Learn the foundation.** Study the relevant course or learning journey.
4. **Recall what matters.** Explain important ideas without looking at the answer.
5. **Practice the real situation.** Enter a Studio Lab for an interview, thesis defense, customer conversation, or another relevant challenge.
6. **Review the feedback.** Identify strengths, gaps, and the moments that deserve another attempt.
7. **Apply the learning.** Use the capability in a real class, project, job, or conversation.
8. **Return and grow.** Revisit important knowledge, collect evidence, and choose the next challenge.

## The promise of Human OS

Human OS is built around a simple belief: technology should help people become more humanly capable.

That means helping people:

- understand more deeply;
- remember more reliably;
- communicate more clearly;
- practice before the moment matters;
- make better judgments;
- learn from experience; and
- become increasingly independent.

Human OS is therefore not only a place to receive information. It is a place to transform information into understanding, understanding into practice, and practice into lasting capability.

# Section 1. Human OS Enterprise

## The Human OS Enterprise HR Console

Human OS Enterprise is the organizational workspace for managers and HR administrators. It brings together the information needed to understand the organization, define roles, support employees, plan learning, and connect business priorities with human capability.

The console is designed for people who guide an organization. It helps them answer practical questions such as:

- Who works in the organization?
- What does each person do?
- Which department and manager are connected to that person?
- What does each role require?
- Which skills, responsibilities, certifications, and processes matter?
- What learning should each employee receive?
- Which capabilities support the organization's goals?
- Where are the most important development gaps?

Human OS Enterprise is intended for **HR administrators, managers, and organizational leaders**. Employees use the employee experience to learn and practice; Enterprise gives authorized leaders the context to organize, support, and develop their people.

## Enterprise navigation at a glance

The Enterprise console is organized into the following areas:

1. **Overview**: A summary of departments, roles, people, and employees without an assigned role.
2. **Organization**: The structure of areas, departments, teams, and reporting relationships.
3. **Departments**: The department directory with leaders, members, locations, missions, and cost centers.
4. **Business Goals**: Vision, mission, values, strategic pillars, goals, and capability alignment.
5. **People**: The employee directory, invitations, assignments, and access to individual profiles.
6. **Roles and Jobs**: Job descriptions and the expectations connected to each role.
7. **Responsibilities**: The catalog of duties and responsibilities that can be connected to roles.
8. **Certifications**: The certifications that may be relevant to roles and development plans.
9. **Capability Catalog**: Job skills, soft skills, department processes, and HR processes.
10. **Process to Learning**: Turning an important work process into structured learning and skill development.
11. **Career Paths**: Personalized development roadmaps for employees.
12. **Gap Reports**: A view of the difference between what a role requires and what people or teams currently demonstrate.
13. **Capability Studio**: The space used to transform approved source material into courses and learning experiences.

The exact information visible to a person depends on their responsibility and permission. HR administrators have a broad organizational view. Managers work with the people and teams they lead. Personal and training information should be handled with care and shared only with people who need it for their responsibilities.

## Chapter 1. Enterprise Overview

The Overview page gives an immediate picture of the organization as a learning and capability environment.

It highlights:

- the number of departments or areas;
- the number of roles and jobs;
- the number of people;
- the number of people who still need a role assignment; and
- upcoming organizational development priorities.

This is the starting point for an HR administrator or manager. It shows whether the organization has a clear structure and where attention may be needed first.

## Chapter 2. Organization and Departments

## Organization

The Organization area represents how the company is arranged. It can show a hierarchy of areas, departments, and sub-departments.

Leaders can:

- create a department or area;
- describe its purpose;
- place it under a larger area;
- add sub-departments;
- edit the structure as the organization changes; and
- review the roles and people connected to each area.

An organization chart can also be provided as a source for creating an initial structure. The result can be reviewed and corrected before it becomes part of the organization's working information.

## Departments

The Departments page provides a directory of organizational units. A department may include:

- its name and code;
- its category;
- its mission or description;
- its leader;
- its members;
- its location; and
- its cost center.

Departments are important because they connect people, roles, processes, goals, and learning. A department is not only an administrative label. It gives learning and capability a meaningful business context.

## Chapter 3. Strategic Goals and Organizational Direction

The Business Goals area helps leaders describe where the organization is going and how people and capabilities contribute to that direction.

## Company direction

Leaders can define:

- the strategic vision, describing where the organization wants to go;
- the organizational mission, describing its daily purpose; and
- the core values that guide conduct and decisions.

## Strategic pillars

Strategic pillars are the main themes that organize growth. Examples may include:

- innovation and technological transformation;
- operational excellence and quality;
- talent and capability growth; and
- customer, financial, or social impact.

## Strategic goals

Each goal can include:

- a title and explanation;
- the strategic pillar it supports;
- the department responsible for contributing;
- a target quarter and date;
- a progress percentage;
- a status such as Active, In Progress, Completed, or Paused; and
- the skills that help make the goal possible.

This connects organizational ambition with practical capability. A goal is not only something leadership writes down. It can be connected to the skills and learning that allow people to accomplish it.

## Chapter 4. People and Employee Profiles

The People area is the organizational directory for employees and other members of the company.

## Finding and organizing people

Leaders can search people by name, email, or role and filter them by department. A person's organizational information may include:

- full name;
- email address;
- phone number;
- address;
- LinkedIn information;
- department;
- job role;
- manager;
- business role, such as Employee, Manager, HR Administrator, or Student; and
- organizational location or region.

## Inviting people

Authorized leaders can invite a new person by providing their email, name, and organizational role. The console also shows the status of invitations so the organization can see who has been invited and which invitations are still pending.

## Employee profile

An employee profile brings together the information needed to support that person. It can include:

- general personal information;
- department and manager assignment;
- business role;
- job role;
- résumé or professional history;
- academic background;
- certifications;
- technical skills;
- soft skills;
- tools and technologies;
- responsibilities;
- work conditions;
- performance indicators;
- professional objective; and
- current training and career plan.

The purpose is to understand the whole person in context. A résumé alone does not describe what a person needs to succeed in a particular role, and a job description alone does not show the experience that person already brings. Human OS Enterprise places those perspectives together.

## Uploading a résumé (Resume)

The employee profile can be used to upload a résumé so the person's professional experience is available as part of their learning context.

The résumé can help leaders and learning activities understand:

- previous roles and employers;
- projects and accomplishments;
- experience with particular subjects;
- existing skills and qualifications; and
- areas where a new role may require preparation.

Uploading a résumé does not replace a conversation with the employee. It provides useful context for a more personal development plan and more realistic practice activities.

## Chapter 5. Job Roles and Job Descriptions

The Roles and Jobs area defines what a position means in the organization.

A job description can include:

- job title;
- department;
- level or seniority;
- purpose of the role;
- job summary;
- key responsibilities;
- daily activities;
- required experience;
- education;
- technical skills;
- soft skills;
- department processes;
- HR processes;
- certifications;
- languages;
- tools or technologies;
- reporting relationship;
- performance indicators;
- schedule and work modality;
- location and travel expectations;
- employment type; and
- salary range and benefits.

This gives the organization a shared description of what success in a role requires. It also creates a foundation for assigning learning, preparing interviews, building career paths, and identifying capability gaps.

## Reviewing an AI-assisted job description

Human OS can help draft or improve a job description. The responsible leader still reviews the proposal, compares it with the organization's expectations, and decides what should be accepted. The final description belongs to the organization, not to the AI.

## Chapter 6. Responsibilities and Certifications

## Responsibilities

The Responsibilities catalog describes concrete duties that people may perform in a role. Examples include coordinating projects, serving customers, preparing reports, managing suppliers, leading a team, improving a process, or supporting compliance.

Connecting responsibilities to roles helps distinguish a title from the actual work performed. It also helps a learner practice the situations they will face in the role.

## Certifications

The Certifications catalog records qualifications that may be relevant to a position or development path. These may include professional, academic, industry, safety, quality, compliance, or technology-related certifications.

Certifications are different from everyday skills. A certification is evidence of a formal qualification, while a skill describes something a person can understand and do.

## Chapter 7. The Capability Catalog

The Capability Catalog organizes the knowledge and abilities the organization wants people to develop.

It includes four important areas:

## Job skills

Knowledge and abilities needed to perform a particular role, such as analysis, engineering, finance, sales, design, operations, or customer support.

## Soft skills

Human behaviors that influence how work is performed, such as communication, active listening, teamwork, leadership, adaptability, critical thinking, accountability, and problem-solving.

## Department processes

The practices and workflows needed within a particular department, such as procurement, inventory, reporting, quality, production, or project coordination.

## HR processes

Shared people and workplace practices, such as onboarding, performance conversations, responsible use of AI, information protection, workplace safety, and professional conduct.

Each capability can have a description, an intended audience, and a relationship to roles or courses. A capability may also have a supporting document that can become learning content.

## Chapter 8. From Work Processes to Learning

The Process to Learning area helps the organization turn an important process document into learning that people can understand and use.

The journey begins when a leader uploads a process document, such as a manual, policy, procedure, or operating guide. Human OS then helps the leader:

1. review the chapters and work steps in the document;
2. identify what a person must know or do at each step;
3. find the relevant skills and learning already available;
4. identify content that is missing or incomplete;
5. prepare a learning activity for the missing content;
6. create an assignment connected to the process; and
7. personalize the assignment for a selected employee.

The leader can review the proposed structure and learning relationship before saving it. This makes a company process more than a document stored on a shelf. It becomes a guided opportunity to develop capability.

## Using a PDF to create a class

A PDF connected to a skill or process can be used as source material for a class in Human OS Studio. The source document gives the learning experience a reliable foundation, while the educator or HR leader remains responsible for reviewing the result and confirming that it reflects the organization's expectations.

## Chapter 9. Career Paths and Employee Development

The Career Paths area helps HR and managers create a practical development route for each employee.

A career path can compare:

- the employee's current skills;
- the skills required by the employee's role;
- the employee's résumé and previous experience;
- the employee's professional objective; and
- the learning available in the organization's catalog.

The resulting plan can recommend learning in four connected tracks:

- **My Role:** knowledge and skills required by the position;
- **Soft Skills:** communication, leadership, collaboration, and other behaviors;
- **Department Processes:** the workflows used by the employee's department; and
- **HR Processes:** shared organizational practices and responsibilities.

Each recommendation can be marked as Pending, In Progress, or Completed. The employee and their authorized leader can therefore see where the person is beginning, what is currently underway, and what has already been developed.

## Personalizing a career path

Career development should not be identical for every person with the same job title. Two employees may have the same role but different experience, goals, strengths, and areas for development.

Human OS Enterprise uses the employee's context to make the path more relevant. A manager can discuss the suggested direction with the employee and use it as a starting point for a real development conversation.

## Chapter 10. Capability Gaps and Reports

Gap Reports help leaders understand where the organization may need additional development.

A gap may appear when:

- a role requires a skill that a person has not yet developed;
- a department process is important but has no learning support;
- a required certification is missing;
- a team has uneven preparation for an important responsibility; or
- a strategic goal depends on capabilities that are not yet strong enough.

The purpose of a gap report is not to blame a person. It is to help the organization choose a useful response, such as learning, practice, coaching, mentoring, clearer responsibilities, or a change in the role description.

## Chapter 11. The Connection to Human OS Learning

Human OS Enterprise describes what the organization and each role need. The learner-facing Human OS experience helps people build those capabilities.

The connection works as a cycle:

1. The organization defines its direction and important goals.
2. Leaders describe departments and roles.
3. Roles are connected to responsibilities, skills, processes, and certifications.
4. Approved documents and source material become courses.
5. Employees receive learning connected to their role and development plan.
6. Employees learn, recall, practice, and demonstrate capability.
7. Managers and HR review progress and identify the next development opportunity.

This creates alignment between business purpose and individual growth. The organization can see why a course matters, and the learner can see how the course connects to their real work.

## Chapter 12. Responsible Use of Employee Information

Human OS Enterprise brings together valuable personal and professional information. That information should be used to support people, not to reduce them to a score or label.

Good use of the Enterprise console means:

- keeping employee information accurate;
- explaining how information is used;
- respecting the privacy of résumés and career plans;
- giving employees a meaningful opportunity to discuss their goals;
- treating AI suggestions as proposals that people review; and
- using learning information to support development rather than punish normal learning gaps.

The best development decision combines the information in the console with human judgment, conversation, context, and care.

## Section 1 summary

Human OS Enterprise is the organizational foundation of Human OS. It connects:

- company direction;
- departments and teams;
- people and employee profiles;
- résumés and experience;
- job roles and responsibilities;
- skills and certifications;
- work processes and learning content;
- career paths and development plans; and
- capability gaps and organizational goals.

Its purpose is to help leaders create the conditions in which people can learn the right things, practice meaningful situations, and grow in a direction that benefits both the individual and the organization.

# Section 2. Human OS Studio — Capability Studio

## What is Human OS Studio?

Human OS Studio, also called **Capability Studio**, is the learning-content workspace of Human OS. It is used to organize company knowledge, design programs, prepare learning experiences, create practical simulations, and connect learning with the capabilities people need.

Studio is where a document, idea, business process, or learning need can become a structured experience for a student, employee, manager, or professional.

The Studio workspace helps educators, HR leaders, subject-matter experts, and program designers answer questions such as:

- What should people learn?
- Who needs to learn it?
- How should the learning be organized?
- Which activities will help people practice?
- Which AI support is appropriate?
- What will the learning cost to prepare and operate?
- How can people demonstrate that they understand and can apply the capability?

## Studio areas at a glance

Human OS Studio includes the following areas:

1. **Catalog**: The library of learning programs and experiences available to people.
2. **Programs**: The place where complete learning programs are designed and maintained.
3. **Program Entities**: The building blocks that make up a program, such as classes, lessons, activities, assessments, and practice experiences.
4. **AI Expenses**: A view of the resources used when AI-supported learning content and activities are prepared or delivered.
5. **Domains and Types**: The subjects, categories, and learning types used to organize the Studio.
6. **AI Agents**: The specialized learning partners that support teaching, practice, review, and content creation.
7. **Film Productions**: The area for planning and organizing videos, scenes, voices, and other visual learning material.
8. **Labs Studio**: The workspace for creating practical simulations and realistic situations in which people can practice.

## Chapter 1. Catalog

The Catalog is the front door to the learning created in Studio. It gives educators and organizational leaders a clear view of the programs and learning experiences available to different audiences.

A catalog item can describe:

- the name of the program;
- the subject or capability it develops;
- the intended audience;
- the expected outcome;
- the level of difficulty;
- the estimated effort or duration;
- the learning activities included; and
- the status of the program, such as being prepared, reviewed, or available.

The Catalog helps people find learning by meaning rather than by file name. A person can look for a capability, a professional need, a business process, or a development goal.

For an organization, the Catalog becomes a shared learning library. It can contain general learning for everyone, role-specific learning, department learning, compliance learning, and advanced development programs.

## Chapter 2. Programs

A Program is a complete learning journey. It gives structure to a subject and guides a learner from an initial understanding toward practical capability.

A program may include:

- an introduction to the subject;
- learning objectives;
- chapters or stages;
- explanations and examples;
- conversations with a learning partner;
- recall and reflection activities;
- practice situations;
- evaluation activities; and
- evidence of progress.

Programs can be designed for a university class, an employee onboarding journey, a leadership pathway, a customer-service curriculum, a professional certification, or a company process.

## Designing a program

The person creating a program decides what the learner should understand, what the learner should be able to do, and how the learner will demonstrate progress.

A strong program does not only provide information. It creates a sequence in which the learner can:

1. understand the context;
2. study the essential ideas;
3. connect the ideas with real work;
4. practice decisions and conversations;
5. receive useful feedback;
6. recall the learning later; and
7. demonstrate the capability in a meaningful situation.

## Chapter 3. Program Entities

Program Entities are the parts that make a complete program possible. They help the creator organize learning without making every experience look the same.

An entity may be:

- a course or class;
- a chapter;
- a lesson;
- a learning objective;
- a reading or source document;
- a video;
- a conversation;
- a simulation;
- a practice activity;
- a reflection;
- an assessment; or
- an evidence or completion requirement.

This structure allows a program to be detailed while remaining understandable. The creator can review each part, improve it, and see how it contributes to the learner's journey.

Program Entities can also be reused. A well-designed explanation, practice activity, or simulation may support more than one program when the learning purpose is similar.

## Chapter 4. AI Expenses

The AI Expenses area helps people responsible for Studio understand the resources involved in preparing and delivering AI-supported learning experiences.

This view can help answer:

- Which programs use AI-supported activities?
- Which learning experiences require more preparation or interaction?
- How are AI resources distributed across programs?
- Which experiences are most valuable in relation to their use?

The purpose is responsible planning. AI-supported learning should be useful, intentional, and sustainable. Understanding its expense helps an organization decide where AI adds the most educational value.

AI expenses should be considered alongside learning quality. The least expensive experience is not always the best experience, and a more interactive experience may be worthwhile when it helps a person practice an important decision or conversation.

## Chapter 5. Domains and Types

Domains and Types provide a common language for organizing the learning library.

## Domains

A domain describes the subject area or world of practice. Examples may include:

- leadership;
- customer service;
- engineering;
- finance;
- education;
- operations;
- communication;
- health and safety;
- sales;
- research; and
- personal development.

Domains help people find related programs and understand where a capability belongs.

## Learning types

A learning type describes the form or purpose of an experience. It may be:

- an explanation;
- a guided lesson;
- a conversation;
- a case study;
- a simulation;
- a practice session;
- a review;
- a reflection;
- an evaluation; or
- a project.

Together, domains and types make the Catalog easier to navigate and help program creators choose the right learning form for the intended outcome.

## Chapter 6. AI Agents

AI Agents in Studio are specialized learning partners. Each one can have a particular role, tone, subject, or responsibility within a learning experience.

An agent may support a learner as:

- a teacher who explains a subject;
- a coach who asks questions and encourages reflection;
- an interviewer who creates a realistic interview;
- a customer who helps a person practice a service conversation;
- a thesis examiner who tests the learner's understanding;
- a manager who discusses a workplace decision;
- a reviewer who gives feedback; or
- a subject specialist who provides context and examples.

The creator defines the purpose of the agent and the kind of experience it should provide. The agent should remain connected to the learning objective rather than becoming a general conversation with no clear educational purpose.

## Responsible use of AI Agents

AI Agents support human learning, but they do not replace educators, managers, or professional judgment. A creator reviews the agent's behavior, language, expectations, and feedback before making the experience available.

The best agent experiences are clear about their role, respectful toward the learner, and focused on helping the person think, practice, improve, and apply knowledge.

## Chapter 7. Film Productions

Film Productions is the Studio area for planning visual learning material.

A film production can organize:

- the purpose of a video;
- the intended audience;
- scenes and sequence;
- speakers or characters;
- narration and dialogue;
- visual references;
- voice and language choices;
- supporting text; and
- the relationship between the video and the program.

Videos can make a situation easier to understand, especially when the learning depends on observing behavior, tone, sequence, environment, or professional standards.

Film material may introduce a process, show a model conversation, present a workplace situation, explain a complex idea, or prepare the learner for a Studio Lab simulation.

The purpose of film is not decoration. A good learning video gives the learner a useful point of view and prepares them to understand or practice something important.

## Chapter 8. Labs Studio

Labs Studio is where practical simulations are created. It helps turn a learning objective into a realistic situation in which the learner must think, speak, decide, and respond.

Examples include:

- practicing an interview with an employer;
- defending a thesis before an examiner;
- speaking with a customer;
- handling a difficult conversation;
- presenting a proposal;
- leading a team meeting;
- explaining a technical or professional decision;
- resolving a service problem; and
- preparing for a real workplace responsibility.

## Building a lab

A Studio Lab can define:

- the situation and its context;
- the learner's role;
- the other person or people in the situation;
- the purpose of the conversation or activity;
- the information available to the learner;
- the challenge or decision;
- the expected behaviors;
- the possible reactions of the other participants;
- the criteria used for feedback; and
- the learning that should happen after the practice.

The learner should feel that the situation matters. The lab can be challenging, but it should also be fair, understandable, and connected to something the learner is preparing to do in real life.

## Feedback from a lab

After a practice session, the learner can receive feedback about areas such as:

- clarity;
- listening;
- reasoning;
- preparation;
- confidence;
- empathy;
- organization;
- professional judgment; and
- alignment with the learning objective.

Feedback should identify what worked, what could improve, and what the learner can try next. The purpose is growth, not embarrassment.

## Chapter 9. Creating a Class from a PDF

Human OS Studio can use a PDF or another source document as the starting point for a class.

The creator can:

1. provide the source material;
2. identify the subject and intended audience;
3. review the important sections and ideas;
4. organize the material into lessons or chapters;
5. add explanations, examples, conversations, and practice;
6. connect the class to a domain and capability;
7. review the learning experience; and
8. make it available through the Catalog.

The source document provides knowledge and context. Studio adds structure, practice, recall, and evaluation so that the learner can use the information rather than only read it.

## Chapter 9A. Creating a Class with Engrams

The **New Structured Learning** area is the Studio Builder for creating a class from the beginning. It helps the creator move from a subject or source document to a complete learning path.

The class can be built with **Engrams**. An Engram is a meaningful unit of learning: an idea, skill, process, decision, behavior, or lesson that a learner should understand and be able to use. Engrams help connect the class to the learner's memory, practice, and future recall.

The Studio Builder guides the creator through six steps:

## 1. Basic information

The creator establishes the identity and purpose of the class.

This step can include:

- the class title;
- a short description;
- the subject or domain;
- the intended audience;
- the level of difficulty;
- the expected learning outcomes; and
- the relationship to a skill, role, process, or development goal.

The basic information answers a simple question: **Who is this class for, and why should they take it?**

## 2. Course design

The creator organizes the learning journey. This is where the main ideas, sequence, and learning objectives are shaped into a coherent class.

The course design may define:

- the opening context;
- the main topics;
- the order in which topics should be learned;
- the Engrams the learner should develop;
- the explanations and examples needed;
- the practice opportunities; and
- the way progress will be recognized.

The goal is to create a learning experience with a clear beginning, development, and conclusion rather than a collection of unrelated materials.

## 3. Material type

The creator identifies the kind of material being used or created. It may be:

- a written explanation;
- a PDF or document;
- a video;
- an image or presentation;
- an interview or conversation;
- a case study;
- a process guide;
- a practical exercise; or
- a simulation.

Choosing the material type helps the creator decide how the learner should encounter the subject. A process may need a demonstration and practice. A theory may need an explanation, examples, questions, and recall.

## 4. Upload content

The creator provides the source material for the class. This may be a PDF, document, presentation, image, video, or other learning resource.

The source material should be reviewed for:

- accuracy;
- clarity;
- relevance to the intended audience;
- permission to use it;
- important sections or chapters; and
- the Engrams it should help the learner develop.

Uploading content is the beginning of the class, not the end. The material becomes more useful when it is connected to explanations, examples, questions, practice, and a learning path.

## 5. Review

Before the class is finalized, the creator reviews what Studio has prepared.

The review can consider:

- whether the class reflects the source material;
- whether the learning objectives are clear;
- whether the Engrams are meaningful and complete;
- whether the sequence makes sense;
- whether the language fits the audience;
- whether examples are understandable;
- whether the class provides enough opportunities to practice; and
- whether the learner will know what to do next.

This is also the moment to correct, remove, or add material. A responsible creator approves only the content that represents the intended educational quality.

## 6. Learning path design

The final step organizes how the learner will experience the class over time.

The learning path can connect:

- introductory information;
- teaching and explanation;
- deeper exploration;
- role-based application;
- recall activities;
- practice or simulation;
- feedback; and
- evaluation.

The path can also show which Engrams should be learned first, which depend on earlier understanding, and where the learner should practice before moving forward.

## Engrams and Graph Learning Lessons

Engrams make it possible to design learning as a connected graph rather than a simple list of pages. Each Engram represents a meaningful piece of understanding or capability, and the relationships between Engrams show how learning develops.

## The learning graph

The graph is a map of how knowledge, skills, people, situations, and learning activities are related.

In a simple list, a learner may see one lesson followed by another lesson. In a graph, the learner can also see why the lessons belong together:

- one fact may explain another fact;
- one skill may depend on an earlier skill;
- one process step may require a particular decision;
- one role may require several capabilities;
- one capability may be practiced in several situations; and
- one practice activity may provide evidence for more than one learning objective.

The graph helps the creator design a learning experience with meaning and direction. It also helps the learner connect new knowledge with what they already understand.

## Facts in a learning experience

A fact is a reliable piece of information that the learner should know, remember, or use. It may be a definition, rule, date, relationship, principle, requirement, process step, or verified observation.

Examples of facts include:

- a customer service policy requires a response within a defined period;
- a laboratory procedure has to be completed in a particular order;
- a historical event occurred in a specific context;
- a role is responsible for a defined outcome;
- a professional term has a particular meaning; or
- a safety practice is required before a task begins.

Facts give a class a trustworthy foundation. They should come from reviewed source material, professional knowledge, institutional standards, or other reliable references. A fact is different from an opinion, a guess, or a conversational suggestion.

## Connecting facts to Engrams

Facts become more useful when they are connected to an Engram and to a situation in which the learner can use them.

For example:

1. A fact explains that a customer request must be recorded.
2. An Engram teaches the learner how to identify the important information in the request.
3. A lesson shows an example of a complete record.
4. A Studio Lab asks the learner to respond to a customer and record the request.
5. Feedback shows whether the learner used the fact correctly and performed the related skill.

This connection moves the learner from knowing a fact to understanding its meaning and applying it responsibly.

## Facts, relationships, and practice

The graph can show different kinds of relationships between facts and Engrams:

- **supports**: a fact provides the foundation for a lesson;
- **explains**: one fact clarifies another idea;
- **depends on**: an Engram requires earlier knowledge;
- **applies to**: a fact or skill is used in a particular role or situation;
- **is practiced by**: a learner activity gives the learner an opportunity to use it;
- **is evaluated by**: an assessment or simulation checks understanding; and
- **is recalled through**: a question, review, or future activity helps the learner remember it.

These relationships help the creator see whether the class is complete. If a fact has no explanation, practice, or opportunity for recall, the learner may know the words without being able to use them. If a practice activity has no supporting facts or capability, it may feel disconnected from the purpose of the class.

## Keeping the graph trustworthy

The graph should be reviewed as carefully as the written material. The creator checks that:

- facts are accurate and current;
- important facts are connected to the right Engrams;
- relationships reflect the real subject or process;
- required knowledge appears before advanced practice;
- learners are not asked to apply information they have not encountered; and
- practice and evaluation reflect the capability the class promises to develop.

The result is a learning structure that is easier to understand, easier to improve, and more useful for long-term recall.

For example, a class about customer conversations might connect the following Engrams:

1. Understand the customer's situation.
2. Listen for the customer's real need.
3. Ask a useful clarifying question.
4. Explain an appropriate solution.
5. Confirm understanding.
6. Agree on the next action.
7. Reflect on the quality of the conversation.

The learner can encounter these Engrams through explanations, examples, questions, practice conversations, and recall activities. The connections help the learner understand not only each idea, but also how the ideas work together in a real situation.

## Designing lessons from Engrams

For each Engram, the creator can consider:

- What should the learner understand?
- What should the learner remember later?
- What should the learner be able to do?
- What example would make the idea clear?
- What question would reveal understanding?
- What situation would allow the learner to practice?
- What feedback would help the learner improve?
- Which other Engrams should come before or after it?

This creates lessons that are connected to a capability rather than lessons that only transfer information. A learner can build one Engram, connect it to the next, and gradually develop a complete way of thinking or working.

## A complete class created with Engrams

A class created in Studio may therefore follow this pattern:

1. Define the purpose and audience in **Basic information**.
2. Arrange the subject and Engrams in **Course design**.
3. Choose the appropriate **Material type**.
4. **Upload content** and connect it to the relevant Engrams.
5. **Review** the explanations, relationships, and activities.
6. Build the learner's **Learning path design**.
7. Make the class available through the Catalog.
8. Let learners study, practice, recall, and demonstrate the connected capability.

The result is a class with structure, memory, practice, and purpose. It can begin with a PDF or another source, but it ends as a guided learning experience that helps people use what they have learned.

## Chapter 10. From Studio to the Learner

Studio and the learner experience work together in a continuous learning cycle:

1. An educator or organizational expert identifies an important capability.
2. Source material, experience, or a business need provides the subject.
3. Studio organizes the subject into a program.
4. Program Entities create lessons, conversations, practice, and evaluation.
5. AI Agents support selected parts of the learning experience.
6. Film Productions provide visual explanations when they are useful.
7. Labs Studio creates realistic practice situations.
8. The program appears in the Catalog for its intended audience.
9. Learners study, practice, recall, and demonstrate what they can do.
10. Educators and leaders improve the program based on the learning experience.

This cycle keeps learning connected to real human needs. A class is not finished simply because its material has been written. It becomes valuable when people can understand it, remember it, apply it, and use it with confidence.

## Chapter 7. Course AI Agents

Course AI Agents are conversational partners connected to a specific course. In Grow With Us, the AI Agents page presents one specialized agent card for each available or enrolled course. Opening a card takes the learner to that course's own agent conversation.

Each course agent is grounded in the Engrams that belong to that course. It does not treat the entire learning catalog as one undifferentiated subject. The agent can use the course's facts, lessons, and connected Engrams while also considering the learner's own objectives, skills, progress, and previous attempts for that course.

During a conversation, the agent can show which Engrams were used in its answer. The learner can open those Engrams to review the related idea, lesson, or knowledge unit. This makes the conversation traceable: the learner can see not only what the agent said, but also which parts of the course supported the answer.

They can support the learner by:

- teaching a concept;
- checking understanding;
- asking the learner to apply an idea;
- simulating a professional situation;
- giving feedback after a response;
- helping the learner prepare for an evaluation; and
- encouraging recall after time has passed.

The difference between a program agent and a course agent is one of focus. A program agent can support the wider journey, while a course agent can concentrate on the subject and activities of one course.

## Course agents and program agents

- **Course AI Agent**: one agent for one course, using that course's Engrams and learning material.
- **Program AI Agent**: one agent for a complete program, able to connect the courses and Engrams that belong to that program.
- **Domain or topic agent**: a broader learning partner that can connect several courses within a subject or subdomain and suggest a learning route.

The learner therefore has different levels of support. A course agent answers questions about one course. A program agent helps connect the complete journey. A domain or topic agent helps the learner understand relationships across several courses.
